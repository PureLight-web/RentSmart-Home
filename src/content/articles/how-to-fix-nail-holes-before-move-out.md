# How to Fix Nail Holes Before Move-Out Without Making It Worse

Before filling nail holes in a rental, check your tenancy agreement and move-out instructions. Some small holes may need only a modest repair, while larger anchors, damaged plaster or paint lift can require more work. The safest strategy is to document the wall, ask what standard is expected, use a suitable repair product only when permitted, and avoid turning a small hole into a larger patch.

## Start by identifying the damage

Not every hole is the same. A tiny picture nail, a screw, a plastic wall anchor and a broken-out chunk of plaster need different approaches. Check whether the hole is clean and shallow, whether the paint is peeling around it, and whether the wall feels damp, soft or powdery. Photograph the condition before touching it.

If the hole was made by you, don't hide the fact from the landlord when a repair is required. If it was already present at move-in, compare it with your inventory photos and report it through the proper process. A clear record may be more useful than an improvised repair that changes the surface.

## Check the tenancy and ask first if uncertain

Review the agreement for damage, fixtures, redecoration and restoration requirements. If the landlord provided a move-out checklist, follow it. Ask whether small nail holes should be left, filled, or restored using a specified method. Do not assume that every landlord prefers the same solution.

If you are in a dispute about whether a hole existed when you moved in or whether a deduction is allowed, keep your evidence and seek local tenant advice rather than relying on a general DIY page. Deposit rules vary by location.

## Repairing a small hole: a cautious sequence

These are general preparation steps, not a promise that every wall can be repaired as a DIY project.

1. **Photograph the wall.** Capture a wide shot showing the location and a close-up of the hole.
2. **Identify the material.** Painted plaster, drywall, masonry, wallpaper and textured finishes need different materials and methods.
3. **Check permission.** If your agreement restricts repairs or the damage is more than superficial, contact the landlord first.
4. **Choose a compatible product.** Read the filler or patch compound instructions and confirm its intended wall material and hole size.
5. **Protect the area.** Keep dust away from people, pets, vents and belongings. Use appropriate protective equipment as instructed.
6. **Prepare only as directed.** Do not widen the hole unnecessarily or scrape away sound paint just to make the repair easier.
7. **Apply the repair material.** Follow the manufacturer's depth, drying and sanding instructions. Larger holes may need a patch or professional repair.
8. **Check the finish.** Paint sheen and colour can be difficult to match; get approval before repainting a whole wall.
9. **Document the result.** Take an after photo and keep the receipt or product details.

Do not sand an unknown old paint coating or disturb material that may contain lead or another hazardous substance. In older housing, ask the landlord or a qualified professional before disturbing suspicious coatings. Stop if the wall is damp, crumbling or mouldy.

## Why improvised fillers can backfire

Household pastes, toothpaste and other improvised mixtures are not designed as wall-repair products. They may shrink, discolour, remain soft, attract dirt or behave differently when painted. A repair can be more obvious than the original tiny hole, especially under side lighting.

Use a repair compound only when it is suitable for the wall material and within the specified hole size. If the wall has texture, a smooth patch may stand out. If there are many holes, the paint may have faded, or the area is high visibility, ask the landlord whether professional patching or painting is preferred.

## Paint matching is harder than it looks

Even when you know the original colour name, the paint on the wall may have aged or been exposed to sunlight. Finish matters too: flat/matt paint reflects light differently from eggshell or satin. A spot of fresh paint can appear as a visible circle because the sheen differs from the surrounding wall.

Do not repaint large sections without approval. Ask whether the landlord has a saved paint code or preferred contractor. If matching is required, document the agreement in writing so expectations are clear.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Filling holes before comparing them with move-in evidence.
- Using toothpaste or improvised paste as a permanent repair.
- Sanding an unknown old coating without checking hazards.
- Filling a damp or crumbling wall instead of reporting the underlying problem.
- Painting a large area without approval.
- Assuming a paint code guarantees an invisible match.
- Forgetting that anchors and larger holes need a different repair.
- Using a DIY article to resolve a legal dispute about deposit deductions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I fix every nail hole?
Check your agreement and move-out instructions. The expected standard and treatment of minor holes may vary by tenancy and jurisdiction.

### Can toothpaste cover a tiny nail hole?
It is not a dependable wall-repair product and may shrink or discolour. Use a purpose-made material only when permitted and suitable for the wall.

### Will a filled hole disappear completely?
Not necessarily. Texture, paint colour, sheen and lighting can make a patch visible.

### What if a hole was already there?
Use move-in photos and inventory records to show the starting condition and report the matter in writing.

### When should I hire a professional?
For large holes, damaged plaster, suspected hazardous coatings, dampness, mould or work you are not permitted to do, ask the landlord and a suitable professional.
